Marine Navigation

Why GPS matters on a moving ship

Marine navigation is the process of planning, directing, and monitoring a vessel's movement from one point to another — safely and efficiently.

It involves fixing the vessel's position, choosing the safest route, and avoiding hazards. It matters because it protects the safety of the vessel and crew, prevents collisions and groundings, improves voyage efficiency, and underpins international seaborne trade.

1.1 — A history of reducing uncertainty

Every era added a tool. None were thrown away.

Navigation has always asked two questions: where am I, and how do I get where I'm going. Each generation answered with a new instrument layered on top of the last.

3000 BCE – 500 CE

Ancient Civilizations

Pilotage and dead reckoning. Phoenician blue-water voyages. Polynesian wayfinding by swell and star path.

500 – 1400 CE

Medieval Navigation

The magnetic compass reaches Europe. Portolan charts turn compass bearings into sailable lines.

1700 – 1900 CE

Scientific Navigation

Harrison's chronometer finally solves longitude. The sextant and nautical almanac complete the toolkit.

1900 – 1990

Industrial & Electronic

Gyrocompass, RADAR, SONAR, LORAN — and TRANSIT, the satellite system GPS grew out of.

1990 – Present

Satellite & Digital

GPS reaches full capability. GLONASS, Galileo, and BeiDou join it, feeding ECDIS and AIS directly.

03 / 12
Architecture

Three segments, one system

GPS lets a receiver anywhere on Earth determine 3-D position, velocity, and precise time — 24 hours a day, in any weather.

Space Segment

Satellites in Medium Earth Orbit, ~20,200 km altitude, ~55° inclination, orbiting in just under 12 hours. Baseline of 24 operational satellites so at least four are visible from almost any point on Earth.

Control Segment

Ground infrastructure that tracks every satellite, corrects clock errors, updates orbital data. Master control station in Colorado Springs, with monitor stations worldwide.

User Segment

Receivers, antennas, and processors aboard ships, aircraft, vehicles, and phones that compute position, velocity, and time from the broadcast signal.

2026 Snapshot

GPS III block (10 satellites) fully launched as of April 2026; the more resilient GPS IIIF generation begins deployment later this year.

1957 → 2026

History and development of GPS

1957

Sputnik 1 is tracked via Doppler shift — scientists at Johns Hopkins APL realize a satellite's position can locate a receiver, and the reverse must also be true.

1959 – 1964

The U.S. Navy's TRANSIT system uses satellites to fix the position of submarines — GPS's most direct ancestor.

1973

Military satellite-navigation efforts are consolidated into the NAVSTAR GPS Program (DoD; Air Force/Navy/Army/DARPA), designed for 24 satellites in Medium Earth Orbit.

1978

The first NAVSTAR (Block I) satellite is launched.

1983

The Korean Air Lines Flight 007 shootdown prompts President Reagan to commit to civilian GPS access.

1989 – 1993

The full Block II constellation is launched; Initial Operational Capability is declared in December 1993.

1995

GPS reaches Full Operational Capability.

2000

Selective Availability is switched off, improving civilian accuracy roughly tenfold overnight.

2005 – 2018

The GPS III / modernization program begins, adding civil signals L2C, L5, and L1C for accuracy, GNSS interoperability, and interference resistance.

2018 – 2026

GPS III satellites (SV01–SV10) launch progressively; the final satellite launches 21 April 2026 with an optical crosslink demo payload and a new digital atomic clock.

Late 2026 →

First GPS IIIF satellites begin launching, adding a boosted M-Code signal, greater anti-jamming resiliency, and a search-and-rescue (SAR) payload.

The core mechanism

How GPS determines a ship's position

Each satellite broadcasts its identity, orbit, and the exact moment the signal was sent. The receiver measures travel time to compute a pseudorange — then solves its position by trilateration.

Fixed position

With ranges to three satellites, a receiver could fix a 3-D position through the geometric intersection of three spheres. In practice a fourth satellite is used simultaneously to solve for the receiver's own clock error — which is why a valid fix normally needs at least four satellites in view.

  • Signals broadcast on L1, L2, L5 — multi-frequency lets receivers remove ionospheric delay.
  • Unaugmented accuracy: on the order of a few meters (FAA cites ~7 m, 95% of the time).
  • Sufficient for open ocean; not always precise enough for harbor approaches on its own — see Section 07.

6.3 — What is chart datum?

Chart Datum is the horizontal reference system used on a nautical chart to define geographic coordinates. It ensures the coordinates a GPS receiver displays correspond accurately to positions shown on the chart — preventing plotting errors and allowing correct integration with ECDIS. WGS 84 (World Geodetic System 1984) is the international standard datum used by GPS and virtually all modern electronic nautical charts.

Position as GPS reports it (WGS 84)
Same coordinates plotted on an old local-datum paper chart
The resulting plotting error — commonly tens to a few hundred meters

This is exactly why the start-up checklist below always includes verifying the receiver's datum setting — a correct GPS fix plotted on the wrong chart datum can still put a mark in the wrong place.

6.2 — Start-up sequence

  • Ensure the antenna has a clear, unobstructed view of the sky.
  • Power on and allow the self-test to complete.
  • Wait for the receiver to acquire satellite signals.
  • Confirm a valid 3-D position fix.
  • Verify the chart datum is set to WGS 84.

6.4 — Safety precautions

  • Never rely on GPS alone — cross-check radar, ECDIS, AIS, visual bearings.
  • Verify chart datum matches WGS 84 at all times.
  • Monitor alarms for signal loss or integrity faults.
  • Keep the antenna unobstructed at all times.

Augmentation, not a new constellation

Differential GPS — what it is, why it exists

A reference station at a precisely known location compares its surveyed position to what GPS alone reports, computes the error, and broadcasts a correction to nearby "rover" ships.

Because the corrected errors are geographically correlated, the correction is strongest close to the reference station — roughly a few tens of centimeters of extra error per 100 km of separation. DGPS accuracy of 1–3 m is strongest exactly where ships need it most: near the coast and in harbor approaches.

Corrections follow the RTCM SC-104 standard, historically broadcast over marine MF radiobeacons (285–325 kHz).

Integrity, not just accuracy

A reference station continuously checks its own known position against what GPS reports — so it can flag a satellite fault within about 15 seconds. Plain GPS has no equivalent self-check.

2026 status check

Is DGPS still used?

  • The U.S. Coast Guard shut down its last maritime DGPS radiobeacon on 30 June 2020 — unaugmented GPS accuracy became good enough on its own, and WAAS offers a modern alternative.
  • Other administrations still operate DGPS beacon networks; IALA Guideline 1152 helps decide whether to keep, reduce, or replace them.
  • SBAS — WAAS, EGNOS, MSAS, GAGAN, SouthPAN — is DGPS's philosophical successor at continental scale, correcting from geostationary satellites instead of a ground beacon.

SOLAS · IMO

On the bridge: integration and regulation

GPS/DGPS position data feeds ECDIS, AIS, autopilot, and radar overlay — with paper charts and visual observation remaining the required independent backup.

SOLAS Chapter V, Regulation 19 requires all ships to carry a GNSS or terrestrial radionavigation receiver. IMO Resolution A.1046(27) governs which systems are formally recognized.

MSC 111 · MAY 2026

The IMO Maritime Safety Committee adopted amendments to A.1046(27) — resolution MSC.599(111) — formally recognizing augmentation systems within the Worldwide Radionavigation System framework, alongside revised BeiDou receiver performance standards.

MARCH 2025

ICAO, ITU, and IMO issue a joint statement calling GNSS jamming and spoofing a "grave concern" for safety, telecommunications, and international commerce.

MID-2025

A GPSPatron / Gdynia Maritime University field study aboard the research vessel Imor records jamming and spoofing off Gdańsk, Poland, June–October 2025 — positioning was unavailable roughly 17% of the time during the most intense period (late June–July), with spoofing targeting GPS and BeiDou signals detected offshore.

MAY–JUNE 2025

GPS interference cited in the grounding of the container ship MSC ANTONIA near Jeddah; a month later the tanker FRONT EAGLE, carrying roughly 2 million barrels of crude, collided with the tanker ADALYNN in the Gulf of Oman.

SEPTEMBER 2025

The European Commission announces a new Galileo anti-spoofing authentication service, encrypted signals for government use, and a planned interference-detection and localization monitoring service beginning in 2026.

JANUARY 2026

Thirteen European coastal states plus Iceland jointly flag growing GNSS interference in the Baltic and North Seas, call on vessels to keep AIS on and accurate, and urge joint development of terrestrial radionavigation alternatives.

2026

The UK's Royal Institute of Navigation, drawing on over 100 sector experts and 300 masters, reports jamming/spoofing now affects "hundreds of vessels daily" and links it to at least two 2025 collisions or groundings (Baltic, Strait of Hormuz, Red Sea) — the same report flags AIS spoofing as an equally serious, compounding threat.

LATE FEBRUARY 2026

Widespread GNSS jamming/spoofing was already affecting the Persian Gulf, Strait of Hormuz, Red Sea, and Gulf of Oman even before separate U.S.–Israel airstrikes on Iran further destabilized the region.

This is precisely why the long-standing bridge rule — ECDIS + radar + AIS + visual observation + paper chart, never GPS alone — is current best practice, actively reinforced by IMO and national maritime authorities in direct response to real, ongoing incidents.

Resilience

Backup and alternative PNT systems

R-Mode (Ranging Mode)

Reuses MF radiobeacons — the same physical network that once carried DGPS corrections — and the emerging VHF Data Exchange System (VDES) as terrestrial ranging "signals of opportunity," independent of satellites entirely. Range of roughly 300 km from an MF beacon is enough to cover an area like the Baltic Sea. The EU-funded ORMOBASS project (Nov 2023 – Oct 2026) is expanding and testing Baltic Sea R-Mode coverage and developing an interference-monitoring service; at NCSR 13 (June 2026) the IMO finalized draft performance standards for shipborne R-Mode receivers, expected before MSC 112 in December 2026.

eLoran

A modernized, high-power, low-frequency terrestrial system — a GNSS-independent alternative for harbor-approach accuracy and precise timing. The UK is restoring and expanding eLoran (the "Chain Odyssey" transmitter network across Northern Ireland, the Shetlands, the Outer Hebrides, East Anglia/Dover, and Southwest Cornwall) and, in May 2026, launched "Urgent Compass," a two-year programme to develop miniaturized eLoran receivers, antennas, and deployable stations. The UK has also formally engaged the IMO (NCSR 13/INF.22, April 2026) to help integrate eLoran into IMO's regulatory framework; South Korea has operated a separate eLoran testbed since 2021.

The historical arc taught in NAV 1 — celestial navigation → chronometer/sextant → gyrocompass/RADAR/SONAR → GPS/ECDIS/AIS — is gaining a further chapter: hybrid PNT, where ships combine GNSS with terrestrial backups precisely because satellite signals alone are no longer fully trustworthy in several high-traffic regions.

eLoran infrastructure

A low-frequency transmitter mast of the kind eLoran modernizes for GNSS-independent timing and harbor-approach positioning.

SBAS / WAAS network

WAAS's ground reference stations, master stations, and geostationary uplinks — the continental-scale successor to ground-beacon DGPS.

Summary

Key takeaways

  • Navigation evolved by addition, not replacement — each era's tools still echo in bridge doctrine today.
  • GPS fixes position via trilateration to at least four satellites across Space, Control, and User segments.
  • DGPS is an augmentation technique, not a separate constellation — it underlies SBAS and RTK today.
  • IMO regulation is actively evolving in 2026 to formally recognize augmentation systems and GNSS-independent backups.
  • Jamming and spoofing are serious, escalating, documented threats — independent cross-checking remains mandatory, not optional.
  • R-Mode and eLoran are the newest chapter in a very old story: adapting the tools when the old ones show their limits.
